Unloading a box wall: classical edges, small networks and SAM, compared

A robot that unloads a trailer has to look at a wall of cardboard and answer one question over and over: where does each box start and stop, and which one can I pull next without bringing the stack down? I compared every kind of method I could run end to end on a CPU: classical edge and plane pipelines on RGB, depth and lidar; two networks trained on synthetic walls; and three zero-shot "segment anything" models. All of them were scored on the same 200 simulated walls with exact ground truth.

Code: github.com/nadurthi/computervision · carton-wall-perception/ — the box-wall simulator, every method, SeamNet and YOLO training, evaluation, figures, trained weights and every result file (requirements.txt, commands in the README).

Main results.
  • On simulated walls, a small network trained on simulated walls wins by a lot. YOLO11n-seg fine-tuned for 10 CPU epochs reaches a panoptic quality (PQ) of 0.92 (0.91 on the hard walls). A 1.1 M-parameter RGB-D U-Net that only predicts seams reaches 0.88 (0.87). The best classical pipeline reaches 0.67 (0.62).
  • On simulated walls, zero-shot foundation models trail far behind. FastSAM gets PQ 0.40 on its own and 0.45 with a depth-based selector. SAM 2.1-tiny with the same selector gets 0.63 on 30 walls. They split boxes into labels and tape, or merge whole stacks, and on this CPU they take 0.6 s (FastSAM) to about 38 s (SAM 2.1-tiny with a 192-point prompt grid) per frame.
  • Depth matters most when seams are faint. On hard walls with barely visible seams, RGB-only SeamNet drops to PQ 0.83 while RGB-D SeamNet holds at 0.87. On standard walls with clearly visible seams they differ by less than 0.01.
  • Lidar alone cannot separate flush boxes at this density. With 3 cm between rings, the best lidar-only method reaches F1 0.16. Projecting the camera network's labels onto the same points gives F1 0.92: segment in the image, measure in 3D.
  • On real photos the ranking flips. Both networks trained only on simulated walls break real boxes into pieces along print, labels and strapping. FastSAM and SAM 2.1, which never saw a simulated wall, give clean per-box masks on the same photos. Simulation was enough to rank methods and to train geometry, but not enough to train appearance.
  • Once a box is found, its pose is easy. Every method that finds a box fits its face to about 0.3° in normal and well under 1 cm (median) in face centre. Finding the right boundary is the whole problem.

1 · What the robot needs

A suction or clamp gripper does not need a pretty mask. It needs, for every box it might pick, the face it will grab (front face for a truck wall), that face's pose and size, and a pick order that will not collapse the wall. So every method here produces instance masks, and a shared back end turns each mask into a plane fit, a face rectangle and a place in the pick order. That back end is identical for all methods, so differences in the results come only from how well each method finds the boundaries between boxes.

2 · A box-wall simulator with exact ground truth

Real datasets for this problem either have no depth (the Stacked Carton Dataset), no box walls (ARMBench totes), or no lidar at all. So I wrote a procedural simulator. It builds walls the way trailers are loaded: columns of cartons 25–62 cm wide and 18–50 cm tall, stacked from the floor, a second row behind, 2–6 product types per wall so neighbours share print and tape, gaps of 0–12 mm, random yaw and tilt, pushed-in boxes, and missing boxes that reveal the row behind. A vectorised ray caster renders RGB, depth with distance-dependent noise and dropouts at discontinuities, and a lidar scan from a sensor mounted above the camera. Every pixel and every lidar point knows which box it belongs to, and every box knows its exact pose.

The hard split shrinks the gaps by 1.8× and adds more yaw and tilt. A random "seam darkness" per wall makes the dark line between flush boxes anything from clearly visible to almost invisible.

3 · Results

Matching uses IoU ≥ 0.5 with Hungarian assignment. Trailer walls, floor and ceiling are not scored, and boxes with fewer than 300 visible pixels are ignored. PQ (panoptic quality) is the sum of IoUs of matched boxes divided by TP + ½FP + ½FN. It rewards finding every box and drawing its boundary well. Pose errors are medians over matched boxes whose front face is mostly visible.

4 · Does depth help?

The surprise was how close RGB-only SeamNet comes to RGB-D SeamNet overall: PQ 0.87 versus 0.88. The simulator draws seams with a random darkness, so the walls can be split by how visible their seams are:

Depth is insurance. Where seams are clearly visible, the RGB network has everything it needs. On hard walls with faint seams, the RGB-only network loses about 0.04 PQ and the RGB-D one loses nothing. Real walls add shrink wrap, glare and dirty cardboard, which make RGB seams less reliable than in this simulator, so I would not ship an RGB-only unloader.

5 · Lidar: geometry yes, boundaries no

The simulated lidar has 80 rings and 640 columns. At 2 m that is 5 mm between columns but 3 cm between rings, and 8 mm of range noise. Lidar-only methods find columns of boxes and recesses, but not individual boxes inside a flush stack. The gap is narrower than the ring spacing, and the whole front of the wall is one plane to within a couple of centimetres.

All lidar methods are scored on points inside the camera's field of view, so the camera-to-lidar transfer is not penalised for boxes the camera cannot see. In a real unloader, lidar earns its place for safety, mapping and recovering dropped boxes. Box boundaries should come from the camera.

6 · From mask to grasp

Each mask is turned into a grasp the same way: RANSAC plane fit (8 mm threshold, SVD refit), a rectangle in that plane whose extents are the 1st–99th percentiles along its axes (so a few leaked pixels don't stretch it), and a pick order of top-down, then nearest first. The per-method pose numbers in the tables are medians over each method's own matches, which flatters a method that only finds easy boxes. Restricting to the 420 boxes that every method below matched removes that effect:

8 · A look at real photos

The simulator is not reality, so I ran the RGB-only methods on six real warehouse photos from the Stacked Carton Dataset. These photos have no depth and no ground truth here, so this is a qualitative check only. Neither trained network had seen a real image.

The result is the opposite of the synthetic benchmark. SeamNet and YOLO, the two best methods on simulated walls, cut real boxes into fragments. Real cartons carry dense print, many labels, plastic strapping and lighting that my simulator never drew, and both networks learned "a sharp line means a seam". FastSAM and SAM 2.1 were trained on a billion real masks. They give one clean mask per box on most of these photos, including the regular pallet in the last row and the strapped cartons in the second. Their failures are the familiar ones: they also segment labels as objects, and they sometimes take a whole layer as one mask.

9 · What I would build

  • Label real data with a foundation model, then distil. Run SAM 2.1 (with the depth-prior selector) offline on a few thousand real frames from the target site, check the labels quickly, and train a small RGB-D instance-segmentation network (YOLO-seg class or a seam U-Net) on them plus the simulated walls. The small network gives the speed and the box prior; the real labels fix the appearance gap that Figure 8 shows.
  • Use classical geometry for the metric part: plane fit, face rectangle, pick order. It is accurate to millimetres once the boundary is right.
  • Use lidar for safety, mapping and recovering dropped boxes. Take box boundaries from the camera.
  • Keep SAM-class models for labelling and fallback, not for the robot's main loop. At 0.6–38 s per frame on a CPU they are too slow, and on their own they don't know what a box is. They are, however, the most robust appearance model available.

10 · Limits

  • The walls are synthetic and the boxes are perfect cuboids. Real cartons bulge, sag and wear shrink wrap. The numbers rank methods; they are not field accuracy.
  • Everything ran on 2 CPU cores. SeamNet trained for 6 epochs, YOLO for 10, and the SAM models were scored on 30 walls per split.
  • The real-photo check is qualitative only, on six photos without depth. It is enough to show that the synthetic ranking does not carry over, but not to measure by how much.

The families differ in where the box prior lives:

  • Classical: in hand-written rules (a seam is a depth jump, a crease, or a thin dark line; a face is planar and rectangular).
  • Trained (SeamNet, YOLO): in the weights, learned from 1,200 synthetic walls whose labels are exact.
  • Zero-shot (FastSAM, SAM): in a generic notion of "object", plus a small geometric selector (§A9) that injects the box prior after the fact.

A2 · Shared geometry layer

Code: boxwall/geometry.py

Camera model. Pinhole, 480×360, fx = fy = 380 px, principal point at the image centre. A pixel (u, v) with depth z back-projects to

X = (u − c_x) z / f_x,   Y = (v − c_y) z / f_y,   Z = z

Hole filling. The simulated depth sensor drops ~0.4 % of pixels at random and ~50 % of pixels next to depth discontinuities, which is where real structured-light and ToF sensors lose returns. fill_depth fills holes with a growing max filter (3, 5, 9, 17 px). The max keeps the far side of a discontinuity, so a dropout at a seam is filled with "gap" depth rather than smeared across both boxes.

Normals. The point image P(u, v) is box-filtered (5×5) and normals are the cross product of central differences:

n = (P(u+1,v) − P(u−1,v)) × (P(u,v+1) − P(u,v−1)),   flipped to face the camera

Plane fit. RANSAC with 3-point hypotheses (60 iterations, 8 mm inlier threshold), then an SVD refit on the inliers. The plane normal is the singular vector with the smallest singular value.

Face rectangle. Inlier points are expressed in an in-plane basis (e₁ = camera x projected onto the plane, e₂ = n × e₁). The rectangle's orientation comes from cv2.minAreaRect, and its extents from the 1st–99th percentiles of the points along its two axes, so a few pixels leaked from a neighbouring face do not stretch it. That gives the face centre, width and height in metres. This one function is used by the evaluator (pose error), by the pick planner, and by the zero-shot selector.

A3 · Classical camera methods

Code: boxwall/classical.py

C1. RGB straight lines

RGB → grey → CLAHE (clip 2, 8×8 tiles) → Fast Line Detector (Canny 40/90, length ≥ 25 px)
    → draw lines 2 px thick as "boundary" → distance transform of the non-boundary area
    → seeds = distance > 4 px → watershed on −distance
    → keep regions with area ≥ 500 px, area ≤ 25 % of the image, rectangularity ≥ 0.7

Rectangularity is region area divided by the area of its minimum-area rectangle. The method assumes every long straight line is a box edge. On a box wall that is false: tape edges, print blocks, label borders and barcode bars are all long straight lines. That assumption is why this method fails, and the failure is instructive.

C2. Depth jumps + normal creases

depth → fill_depth → normals
boundary = jump ∪ crease ∪ dropout, dilated 3×3
  jump(u)    : |z(u) − z(u')| > 0.012·z + 4 mm, for u' in 4 neighbour directions
  crease(u)  : angle(n(u), n(u')) > 22°, u' two pixels right / down
  dropout(u) : raw depth == 0
seeds = connected components of ¬boundary
labels = watershed(|∇z|, seeds)      grow seeds back across the boundary band
keep regions with 300 px ≤ area ≤ 25 % image and a plane fit with ≥ 60 % inliers (10 mm)

The jump threshold scales with z because depth noise grows with distance (σ ≈ 1.2 mm · z² in the simulator, which is typical of active stereo). It was tuned on the validation split (0.008 / 0.012 / 0.02). Sensor dropouts are treated as boundaries on purpose: they concentrate exactly on discontinuities, so "no return" is evidence of an edge.

What it cannot see: two boxes sitting flush (same depth, same orientation, gap narrower than one depth pixel, ~5 mm at 2 m) produce no jump and no crease. They come out as one region.

C3. Depth edges + RGB seam valleys

C2 merges flush boxes, and RGB contains the cue that separates them: the seam between two cartons is a thin dark line (a shadowed gap, or crushed, darker cardboard at the edges). Tape and print edges are steps, bright on one side and dark on the other, not valleys. C3 adds a valley detector:

grey → Gaussian 3×3 → black-hat (closing − image) with a 9×9 kernel
     → normalise → Fast Line Detector on the black-hat image (length ≥ 30 px)
     → keep lines whose median black-hat response (3×3 max) > 8 grey levels
for each C2 region: cut along accepted seam lines (extended 6 px at both ends)
     → if ≥ 2 pieces of ≥ 300 px remain, re-grow them to fill the region (watershed)
re-apply the planarity / size filter

The black-hat responds only to dark structures narrower than the kernel, so it suppresses tape edges (a step), large dark prints (wider than 9 px) and smooth shading. The threshold was swept on validation (4–25 grey levels). It is flat between 4 and 8; 8 was kept.

An earlier version scored lines found on the plain grey image with a valley-versus-step profile test. That barely changed the result: line detectors fire on the edges of a dark band rather than its centre, so the profile was sampled in the wrong place. Detecting lines on the black-hat image fixed this.

A4 · Lidar methods

Code: boxwall/lidar.py

The simulated lidar is a scanning unit mounted 35 cm above the camera: 80 rings over −38°…+30° elevation and 640 columns over ±48° azimuth, with 8 mm range noise and 2 % random dropouts. At 2 m that is about 5 mm between columns but about 3 cm between rings. That anisotropy decides what lidar can and cannot resolve.

L1. Range-image cuts

scan → range image R (80×640) and point image P (80×640×3)
fill single dropouts from neighbours (max filter, keeps the far side)
median 3×3 on R
jump(u) : |R(u) − R(u')| > 0.015·R + 10 mm, u' = next column, next ring, 2 columns over
crease  : normals from anisotropic differences (±4 columns, ±1 ring) after a 9×3 blur; angle > 80°
seeds = components of ¬boundary, opened with a 1×5 element; watershed back over the scan
drop segments with < 30 points or > 25 % of the scan

With the normal threshold at 25° the segmentation fragmented badly (F1 0.08 on 8 scans), because 8 mm range noise on 5 mm column spacing makes per-point normals unreliable. Raising it to 80° effectively makes the method jump-only, which raised F1 to 0.13. The method finds columns of boxes and recesses well, and almost never finds individual boxes inside a flush stack.

L2. Sequential RANSAC planes + DBSCAN

repeat up to 80 times on the remaining points:
    plane = Open3D segment_plane(distance 15 mm, 3-point RANSAC, 200 iterations)
    split the plane's inliers with DBSCAN (ε = 35 mm, min 8 points)
    each cluster with 30 … 25 %·N points is one instance; remove the inliers

This is the textbook approach from container-unloading papers. Here it has two failure modes. The whole front of the wall is one plane to within ±2 cm, so RANSAC takes many boxes at once. Ring spacing (~3 cm) is close to ε, so DBSCAN bridges any gap narrower than about 3 cm.

L3. Camera → lidar label transfer

project every lidar point into the camera:  u = X/Z·f_x + c_x,  v = Y/Z·f_y + c_y
accept the label at (u, v) only if |z_camera(u, v) − Z| < 4 cm   (the point is the surface the camera saw)

Here lidar is the geometry sensor and the camera supplies instance boundaries. This is the cheapest kind of fusion and the one used in most deployed unloaders: segment in the image, measure in 3D.

A5 · SeamNet: a small RGB-D U-Net that segments seams

Code: boxwall/seamnet.py, training: scripts/train_seamnet.py

The idea is the one the classical C3 approximates by hand: predict the seams, not the boxes. A pixel is background (trailer or ignored), box interior, or seam. Instances are what is left between seams. This turns instance segmentation into 3-class semantic segmentation, which a small fully convolutional network does well and cheaply. The network never has to count boxes or regress masks.

Input encoding (8 channels at 320×240)

ChannelsContentWhy
0–2RGB / 255 − 0.5appearance: seam darkness, print, tape
31 / max(z, 0.3 m) − 0.5inverse depth: well conditioned near the sensor, bounded far away
4–6surface normal (x, y, z) from filled depthmakes creases and yawed faces explicit, so the network doesn't have to differentiate depth itself
7fraction of valid raw-depth pixels − 0.5dropouts mark discontinuities

The RGB-only variant uses channels 0–2. Everything else is identical, so the gap between the two is the value of depth.

Layers (measured, input 1×8×240×320)

Each block is [Conv3×3 → BN → ReLU] × 2. Encoder blocks are joined by 2×2 max-pooling. Decoder blocks take the bilinearly upsampled lower level concatenated with the encoder skip at the same resolution.

Compute is 7.2 GMAC per frame, about 30 % of it in e1 and d1 at full resolution. The bottleneck has stride 8 and a receptive field of about 70 px at the network input (about 100 px after the decoder), roughly one small box face. That is enough to see both sides of a seam and decide whether it is a seam or a tape edge. It is not enough to reason about a whole stack, and it doesn't need to.

Edge deployment notes: every operation is Conv/BN/ReLU/max-pool/bilinear-resize/concat, which all map directly to TensorRT. BN folds into the convolutions, and the weights are 2.2 MB in FP16. Its latency on a Jetson has not been measured here.

Targets and loss

lab  = instance ids resized to 320×240 (nearest)
seam = pixels within 2 px of a different id (max filter ≠ min filter over 5×5), on boxes only
target = 0 background/trailer, 1 interior, 2 seam
loss = cross-entropy with class weights (1, 1, 3)

The seam class is about 18 % of box pixels (measured on the validation set), so it gets 3× weight. A 2 px band at 320×240 is about 3 px at full resolution, close to the visual width of a real seam.

Training

AdamW (lr 2·10⁻³, weight decay 10⁻⁴), one-cycle schedule (10 % warm-up), batch 8, 6 epochs over 1,200 synthetic walls. Augmentation: horizontal flip (the normal-x channel is negated with the image), brightness gain 0.7–1.3, offset ±0.12, per-channel tint ±0.05. About 7 minutes per epoch on 2 CPU cores. The data is cached once as float16 (1.5 GB).

Decoding

interior = softmax[1] > 0.5, opened 3×3; seeds = its connected components (≥ 25 px)
labels   = watershed(softmax[2] as elevation, seeds, mask = ¬background)
upsample labels to 480×360 (nearest)

The watershed runs uphill on seam probability, so each seed grows until it meets the crest of a seam. The mask lets a seed reclaim the seam band on its own side. If a seam is broken for a few pixels, the two interiors touch and become one seed. That merge is the main failure mode.

A6 · YOLO11n-seg: one-stage instance segmentation

  • C3k2: a CSP block. Split channels, run a chain of bottlenecks on one half, concatenate, 1×1 fuse. "k2" means two inner blocks; in the deeper stages those inner blocks are themselves small CSP blocks (C3k).
  • SPPF: three chained 5×5 max-pools, so receptive fields of 5, 9 and 13 cells at stride 32. At 480 px input that is the whole wall.
  • C2PSA: a CSP block wrapping multi-head self-attention over the 15×15 = 225 tokens of P5. It is the only global-context operator in the network.

Top-down, then bottom-up again, so every output level has both semantics and localisation.

Segment head (717,680 params with 80 classes; 1 class after fine-tuning)

For each of the 60² + 30² + 15² = 4,725 anchor points it predicts:

  • box: 4 sides × 16 bins (distribution focal loss, reg_max = 16), a discrete distribution over distance to each side;
  • class score: 1 logit after fine-tuning (80 on COCO);
  • mask coefficients: 32 numbers.

Separately, a Proto module on N3 (Conv 3×3 → ConvTranspose 2×2 s2 → Conv 3×3 → Conv 1×1) outputs 32 prototypes at 120×120 (stride 4). After NMS each surviving box gets its mask:

mask_k = sigmoid( c_k · Proto ) cropped to box_k,   c_k ∈ ℝ³²,  Proto ∈ ℝ^{32×120×120}

Training assigns anchors to ground truth with the task-aligned assigner (classification score × IoU). Losses are CIoU + DFL for boxes, BCE for classes, and per-pixel BCE for masks inside each GT box.

Why it suits boxes, and where it can fail: a carton face is a near-rectangle, the shape that box regression and box-cropped masks handle best. Cropping each mask to its own box also stops it from leaking into the neighbour, which is SeamNet's main failure. In these experiments YOLO was the most accurate method, with the fewest merges.

The structural risks are still worth knowing for real data:

  • masks are assembled at stride 4 from 32 shared prototypes, so a mask edge is only as sharp as those prototypes allow;
  • NMS can drop one of two adjacent, similar boxes whose predicted boxes overlap heavily, for example strongly yawed boxes or a box partly hidden behind another.

It also starts from COCO weights, whereas SeamNet starts from scratch. That is part of why 10 epochs were enough.

C2f is the YOLOv8 CSP variant: split, run n bottlenecks, and concatenate every intermediate output before the 1×1 fuse. It gives richer gradients than C3k2 at the cost of more channels.

"Segment everything" is just running the detector with a low confidence threshold (0.25) and keeping everything after NMS (IoU 0.7). On a typical wall it returns around 125 masks: whole boxes, box faces, labels, tape strips, print blocks and groups of boxes, all with the same class. Turning that soup into one mask per carton is the selector's job (§A9).

Hiera is a hierarchical ViT with no convolutions inside the stages. Downsampling is query pooling at the first block of stages 2–4. Most attention is windowed, and three global-attention blocks in stage 3 give whole-image context. Unlike the original SAM decoder, SAM 2's decoder also receives the stride-4 and stride-8 features from the FPN, so mask edges are refined from high-resolution features rather than only upsampled from 64×64. SAM 2 also has memory attention (5.9 M), a memory encoder (1.4 M) and object-pointer projections for video. They are not used for single images, so the active image path is about 31.4 M parameters.

Prompt encoder (6,220 params, shared design)

A point prompt (x, y, label) becomes a 256-d token: a random-Fourier-feature positional encoding of (x, y) plus a learned embedding for "foreground point". A learned dense positional encoding is added to the image embedding.

Mask decoder (MobileSAM 4.06 M, SAM 2.1-t 4.22 M)

tokens = [IoU token, 4 mask tokens (SAM 2 adds an object-score token)] + prompt tokens
two-way transformer, 2 layers, 256-d, 8 heads:
    token self-attention → tokens attend to image → MLP → image attends to tokens
upscale image embedding ×4 with 2 transposed convs  (SAM 2: adds stride-8 / stride-4 FPN features)
each mask token → 3-layer MLP → dynamic 1×1 filter over the upscaled features → mask logits (256×256)
IoU token → MLP → predicted IoU for each mask

A single point is ambiguous (a label? the face? the whole box? the stack?), which is why the decoder emits three masks: roughly part, object and whole. The best-scoring one per prompt is kept.

How they are run here

There is no detector, so prompts come from a regular 16 × 12 grid of 192 foreground points. The image is encoded once (set_image), then prompts are decoded in chunks of 16 (decoding all 192 at once at 1024² needed more than the 8 GB RAM available). This is a reduced version of SAM's "automatic mask generator", which uses a 32×32 grid, crops and stability filtering.

A9 · Turning "segment everything" into boxes

Code: select() in boxwall/zeroshot.py

The zero-shot models know what an object is, not what a carton is. The selector adds that prior.

for every candidate mask:
    reject if area < 300 px or > 20 % of the image
    reject if rectangularity < 0.75
    [RGB-D only]
    fit a plane (RANSAC, §A2): reject if < 60 % inliers        → one flat face, not a corner or a group
    fit the face rectangle: reject unless 8 cm ≤ width, height ≤ 70 cm   → physically carton-sized
sort survivors by area, largest first
paint each into the label map if ≥ 70 % of it is still unclaimed

"Largest first" prefers whole boxes over labels and tape. The plane and size tests reject the multi-box masks that "largest first" would otherwise also prefer. The size range is the one real prior: it is the range of cartons the gripper handles, which a deployed system knows. Without depth (the _rgb variants), only area and rectangularity are available, so a stack of two boxes or a large label passes as easily as a single box.

A10 · From a mask to a grasp

Code: pick_plan() in scripts/make_figures.py

For every predicted instance:

  1. Fit the face plane and rectangle (§A2). Keep faces whose normal is within ~30° of the sensor axis (nz < −0.85); those are suction-pickable from the front.
  2. Report the pick as face centre + normal (approach direction = −n) and width × height, which bounds the box and sizes the suction cup array.
  3. Order picks top-down, then nearest-first. Faces are bucketed by the height of their top edge in 10 cm bands, and within a band the smallest z goes first. That never pulls a box from under another.

The evaluation reports the median errors of steps 1–2 against ground truth (normal angle, face-centre distance, size) for every correctly matched box whose front face is mostly visible.
