Spin stabilization and detumbling: a deep dive

A spinning body resists being tipped over. That one property kept the first satellites pointed, keeps a thrown football on its axis and keeps a spin-stabilized upper stage steady through a long burn. It also hides traps. A body spinning about the wrong axis can flip over with no torque at all, and a satellite that is a perfectly stable spinner on paper can end up tumbling end over end because its antennas flex. This page works through the rigid-body physics behind both effects and then turns to control: how to bring a tumbling spacecraft to rest with thrusters or wheels, which gain is optimal and why, and how a CubeSat does it with nothing but magnetic coils.

1 · The rigid body and Euler's equations

Put the body frame on the principal axes, so the inertia matrix is diagonal, $I=\operatorname{diag}(I_1,I_2,I_3)$. We order the axes so that $I_1>I_2>I_3$: axis 1 is the major axis, axis 2 the intermediate axis and axis 3 the minor axis. Throughout the page $I=\operatorname{diag}(14,10,8)\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$, the example used in the HJB paper.

The angular momentum is $\mathbf H = I\boldsymbol\omega$. In an inertial frame Newton's law for rotation is simply $\dot{\mathbf H}_{\text{inertial}}=\boldsymbol\tau$. Written in the rotating body frame, the derivative picks up a transport term $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf H$, which gives Euler's equations:

$$I\dot{\boldsymbol\omega} = (I\boldsymbol\omega)\times\boldsymbol\omega + \boldsymbol\tau \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \begin{aligned} I_1\dot\omega_1 &= (I_2-I_3)\,\omega_2\omega_3 + \tau_1\\ I_2\dot\omega_2 &= (I_3-I_1)\,\omega_3\omega_1 + \tau_2\\ I_3\dot\omega_3 &= (I_1-I_2)\,\omega_1\omega_2 + \tau_3 \end{aligned}$$

The attitude follows from the angular velocity. With a scalar-last unit quaternion $\mathbf q$ (body to inertial),

$$\dot{\mathbf q} = \tfrac12\,\mathbf q\otimes\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol\omega\\0\end{bmatrix}.$$

The rate equation does not depend on the attitude unless the torque does. So for torque-free motion and for every controller in §6–8 we can study $\boldsymbol\omega$ on its own. The quaternion is only needed to draw the body and, in §9, to rotate the Earth's magnetic field into body axes.

2 · What "spin-stabilized" means

Spin a body about a principal axis with rate $\Omega$, so $\mathbf H = I_s\Omega\,\hat{\mathbf e}_s$, and apply a small disturbance torque $\boldsymbol\tau_d$ perpendicular to $\mathbf H$. Since $\dot{\mathbf H}=\boldsymbol\tau_d$ in inertial space, the momentum vector does not tip toward the torque. It moves sideways, perpendicular to both, at the precession rate

$$\dot\theta \approx \frac{\|\boldsymbol\tau_d\|}{I_s\Omega}.$$

The spin axis therefore drifts at a rate inversely proportional to the stored momentum, and a disturbance that would tumble a non-spinning body in minutes only slowly walks the axis of a spinner. With $I_s=14\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ and a 60 rpm spin ($\Omega=2\pi$ rad/s), a solar-pressure torque of $10^{-5}$ N·m drifts the axis by about 0.6° per day. That gyroscopic stiffness is all spin stabilization is. It needs no sensors, no actuators and no power, which is why Explorer 1, the early Intelsat and GOES satellites, Pioneer 10/11 and most solid-rocket upper stages were spinners. The price is that only one axis is pointed, and the rest of this page is about the conditions under which that one axis actually stays put.

3 · Torque-free motion on the momentum sphere

With $\boldsymbol\tau=0$, two quantities are conserved. Dotting Euler's equation with $\boldsymbol\omega$ and with $I\boldsymbol\omega$ kills the gyroscopic term both times, because $(I\boldsymbol\omega)\times\boldsymbol\omega$ is perpendicular to both:

$$2T = \boldsymbol\omega^{\mathsf T}I\boldsymbol\omega = \frac{H_1^2}{I_1}+\frac{H_2^2}{I_2}+\frac{H_3^2}{I_3}, \qquad \|\mathbf H\|^2 = H_1^2+H_2^2+H_3^2 .$$

In body-frame momentum coordinates the first is an ellipsoid with semi-axes $\sqrt{2T I_i}$, and the second is a sphere of radius $\|\mathbf H\|$. The body-frame momentum vector must lie on both, so it moves along their intersection curve, a polhode. The energy ranges from $T_{\min}=H^2/2I_1$ (pure spin about the major axis, where the ellipsoid touches the sphere from inside at $\pm\hat{\mathbf e}_1$) to $T_{\max}=H^2/2I_3$ (pure spin about the minor axis).

Two things follow directly from the picture. Near the major and minor axes the polhodes are small closed loops, so a slightly perturbed spin about those axes stays close to them. Near the intermediate axis, the separatrix runs right through the equilibrium: a small perturbation puts the momentum on a curve that carries it all the way around the sphere to the opposite pole.

4 · The intermediate-axis theorem

Linearize about pure spin $\boldsymbol\omega=\Omega\hat{\mathbf e}_i$. Write $\boldsymbol\omega = \Omega\hat{\mathbf e}_i + \delta\boldsymbol\omega$ and let $j,k$ be the other two axes. To first order $\delta\omega_i$ is constant and the transverse rates obey

$$I_j\,\delta\dot\omega_j = (I_k-I_i)\,\Omega\,\delta\omega_k,\qquad I_k\,\delta\dot\omega_k = (I_i-I_j)\,\Omega\,\delta\omega_j \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \delta\ddot\omega_j = \lambda^2\,\delta\omega_j,\quad \lambda^2=\Omega^2\frac{(I_i-I_j)(I_k-I_i)}{I_jI_k}.$$

If $I_i$ is the largest or the smallest inertia the two factors in the numerator have opposite signs, $\lambda^2<0$, and the perturbation oscillates. This is the nutation (wobble) of a stable spinner. If $I_i$ is the intermediate inertia, both factors have the same sign, $\lambda^2>0$, and the perturbation grows exponentially. For our body at $\Omega=1$ rad/s:

Nothing in the instability needs a torque, a flaw in the body or numerical error. It is pure geometry of the momentum sphere, and every rigid body with three distinct principal inertias has it.

5 · Energy dissipation and the major-axis rule

Real spacecraft are not rigid. Sloshing propellant, flexing antennas, nutation dampers and even heat-pipe fluid move relative to the main body. Internal motion exchanges momentum only within the spacecraft, so $\|\mathbf H\|$ is still conserved, but friction in that motion turns kinetic energy into heat, so $T$ only goes down.

A simple model that has exactly these properties is the internal torque

$$\boldsymbol\tau = k\,\mathbf H\times(\mathbf H\times\boldsymbol\omega),\qquad \frac{d\|\mathbf H\|^2}{dt} = 2\mathbf H\cdot\boldsymbol\tau = 0,\qquad \dot T = \boldsymbol\omega\cdot\boldsymbol\tau = -k\left(\|\mathbf H\|^2\|\boldsymbol\omega\|^2-(\mathbf H\cdot\boldsymbol\omega)^2\right)\le 0 .$$

On the momentum sphere this means the momentum vector slides across polhodes toward lower energy. The lowest energy on the sphere is $H^2/2I_1$, at the major axis. So with any dissipation at all, the only stable spin is about the major axis. A minor-axis spinner, stable in rigid-body theory, slowly crosses the separatrix and ends up in a flat spin.

Explorer 1 (1958). The first U.S. satellite was a long, thin pencil spun about its long axis, which is its minor axis. Four flexible whip antennas dissipated energy, and the satellite was soon nutating with a growing cone angle until it settled into a flat spin, end over end about its major axis. The episode is how the major-axis rule entered spacecraft design. A passive spinner must spin about its major axis, or, if it must spin about the minor axis, carry an active nutation damper or a despun section (a dual-spin spacecraft) whose damping flips the sign of the energy argument.

6 · Detumbling: rate damping and its Lyapunov function

Now add a control torque, from thrusters or reaction wheels, with the goal of bringing $\boldsymbol\omega$ to zero. This is detumbling, the first thing a spacecraft does after separation from the launcher. The simplest law is rate damping,

$$\mathbf u = -k\,\boldsymbol\omega,\qquad k>0 .$$

The kinetic energy is a Lyapunov function. Using Euler's equation and the fact that the gyroscopic term is perpendicular to $\boldsymbol\omega$,

$$\dot T = \boldsymbol\omega^{\mathsf T}I\dot{\boldsymbol\omega} = \boldsymbol\omega^{\mathsf T}\big[(I\boldsymbol\omega)\times\boldsymbol\omega\big] + \boldsymbol\omega^{\mathsf T}\mathbf u = -k\|\boldsymbol\omega\|^2 .$$

The nonlinear coupling drops out exactly, so the result is global: any initial tumble, however fast, decays. Since $2T/I_{\max}\le\|\boldsymbol\omega\|^2\le 2T/I_{\min}$, the energy is sandwiched between two exponentials,

$$T_0\,e^{-2kt/I_{\min}}\;\le\;T(t)\;\le\;T_0\,e^{-2kt/I_{\max}} .$$

7 · The optimal detumbling law

Which gain? Pose the problem as optimal control with a quadratic cost on rate and torque,

$$J = \int_0^\infty \left(q\,\|\boldsymbol\omega\|^2 + r\,\|\mathbf u\|^2\right)dt .$$

The Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation for the value function $V(\boldsymbol\omega)$ is

$$0=\min_{\mathbf u}\Big[q\|\boldsymbol\omega\|^2+r\|\mathbf u\|^2+\nabla V^{\mathsf T}I^{-1}\big((I\boldsymbol\omega)\times\boldsymbol\omega+\mathbf u\big)\Big], \qquad \mathbf u^* = -\tfrac{1}{2r}I^{-1}\nabla V .$$

Try the kinetic-energy shape $V = c\,\boldsymbol\omega^{\mathsf T}I\boldsymbol\omega$. Then $\nabla V = 2cI\boldsymbol\omega$, the gyroscopic term contributes $2c\,\boldsymbol\omega^{\mathsf T}[(I\boldsymbol\omega)\times\boldsymbol\omega]=0$ again, $\mathbf u^*=-(c/r)\boldsymbol\omega$, and the HJB equation collapses to $(q - c^2/r)\|\boldsymbol\omega\|^2 = 0$. Hence (Debs & Athans, 1969)

$$\boxed{\;V(\boldsymbol\omega)=\sqrt{qr}\;\boldsymbol\omega^{\mathsf T}I\boldsymbol\omega,\qquad \mathbf u^* = -\sqrt{q/r}\;\boldsymbol\omega\;}$$

The optimal law for this nonlinear system is plain rate damping with gain $k^*=\sqrt{q/r}$, and its value function is twice $\sqrt{qr}$ times the kinetic energy. It is also exactly what LQR gives on the linearization about rest ($A=0$, $B=I^{-1}$): the Riccati equation $PBR^{-1}B^{\mathsf T}P=Q$ has the solution $P=\sqrt{qr}\,I$. Linearization usually loses something; here it loses nothing, because the only nonlinearity is the gyroscopic term and it does no work on $V$.

The same trick gives the cost of any gain in closed form. With $\mathbf u=-k\boldsymbol\omega$, $\frac{d}{dt}(\boldsymbol\omega^{\mathsf T}I\boldsymbol\omega) = -2k\|\boldsymbol\omega\|^2$, so $\int_0^\infty\|\boldsymbol\omega\|^2dt=\boldsymbol\omega_0^{\mathsf T}I\boldsymbol\omega_0/2k$ and

$$J(k)=\frac{q+rk^2}{2k}\;\boldsymbol\omega_0^{\mathsf T}I\boldsymbol\omega_0,$$

which is minimized at $k=\sqrt{q/r}$ with $J^*=V(\boldsymbol\omega_0)$.

This is the problem used as the second example on the HJB sparse-collocation page. Given 84 candidate monomials in $(\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3)$ and no hint about the answer, the ℓ1 collocation method keeps exactly the three terms $\omega_1^2,\omega_2^2,\omega_3^2$ with coefficients $\sqrt{qr}\,I_i$, which is a good test that the numerical method finds structure rather than imposing it.

8 · When torque is limited

Thrusters and wheels have a maximum torque. Two common responses are to saturate the linear law per axis, $u_i=-u_{\max}\tanh(k\omega_i/u_{\max})$, or to go fully bang-bang, $u_i=-u_{\max}\operatorname{sign}(\omega_i)$. Both keep the Lyapunov argument, since $\dot T=\boldsymbol\omega^{\mathsf T}\mathbf u$ and each term $\omega_iu_i$ is still non-positive. Bang-bang gives something stronger. Because $\|\boldsymbol\omega\|_1\ge\|\boldsymbol\omega\|_2\ge\sqrt{2T/I_{\max}}$,

$$\dot T = -u_{\max}\|\boldsymbol\omega\|_1 \le -u_{\max}\sqrt{2T/I_{\max}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d\sqrt T}{dt}\le -\frac{u_{\max}}{\sqrt{2I_{\max}}},\qquad t_f\le\frac{\sqrt{2I_{\max}T_0}}{u_{\max}},$$

so the body stops in finite time. For our case, $T_0=7.53$ J and $u_{\max}=1$ N·m, the bound is 14.5 s, and the simulation stops at about 10 s.

The optimal law has the lowest cost by construction, but it is neither the fastest (a high gain is) nor the gentlest on the actuator (the torque-limited laws are). The weights $q$ and $r$ are the dial between those, and the torque-limited laws show that with a hard actuator limit a constant-magnitude torque, switched by the sign of each rate, is both simple and quick.

9 · Magnetic detumbling: the B-dot law

Small satellites usually detumble with magnetorquers: coils that create a magnetic dipole $\mathbf m$ and feel a torque $\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf m\times\mathbf B$ from the Earth's field. The catch is visible in the cross product. No torque can be made along $\mathbf B$, so the system is underactuated at every instant and only becomes controllable on average as the field direction changes around the orbit.

The classic answer is B-dot control, which needs only a magnetometer. In the body frame the measured field direction $\hat{\mathbf b}=\mathbf B/\|\mathbf B\|$ changes mostly because the body rotates, $\dot{\hat{\mathbf b}}\approx-\boldsymbol\omega\times\hat{\mathbf b}$, so its derivative is a rate measurement in disguise. The normalized law of Avanzini & Giulietti (2012) commands

$$\mathbf m = -\frac{k}{\|\mathbf B\|}\,\dot{\hat{\mathbf b}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boldsymbol\tau = \mathbf m\times\mathbf B \approx -k\left(\mathbf 1_3-\hat{\mathbf b}\hat{\mathbf b}^{\mathsf T}\right)\boldsymbol\omega ,$$

which is rate damping on the components of $\boldsymbol\omega$ perpendicular to the field, with $\dot T\approx-k\|\boldsymbol\omega_\perp\|^2\le0$. Their recommended gain is $k=2n(1+\sin\xi)I_{\min}$, where $n$ is the orbital mean motion and $\xi$ the inclination to the geomagnetic equator.

The simulation uses a 6U-class CubeSat, $I=\operatorname{diag}(0.10,0.08,0.05)$ kg·m², with coils saturating at 0.2 A·m², in a 500 km sun-synchronous orbit (97.4°, period 94.5 min) through a tilt-free dipole field of $3.12\times10^{-5}$ T at the equatorial surface. The magnetometer derivative includes the rotation of the field along the orbit, as a real finite-difference measurement would.

Why $2n$? In a near-polar orbit the dipole field direction rotates through 720° in inertial space each orbit: horizontal over the equator, vertical over the poles, horizontal again but reversed over the other side. B-dot damps the body's rotation relative to the field, so the body ends up following the field, which turns at twice the orbital rate. That is a feature for the next phase of the mission: a slowly rotating, well-damped body is exactly the starting condition for an attitude-determination filter and for the pointing controller that takes over.

10 · Takeaways

  • Spin about the major axis. Rigid-body theory allows major or minor axis spins, the intermediate axis never. Energy dissipation, which every real vehicle has, rules out the minor axis too.
  • The gyroscopic term does no work. That one fact makes kinetic energy a global Lyapunov function, makes rate damping globally stable, and makes the nonlinear optimal law identical to the LQR law.
  • The optimal detumbling law is $\mathbf u=-\sqrt{q/r}\,\boldsymbol\omega$, with value $\sqrt{qr}\,\boldsymbol\omega^{\mathsf T}I\boldsymbol\omega$ and cost $J(k)=\frac{q+rk^2}{2k}\boldsymbol\omega_0^{\mathsf T}I\boldsymbol\omega_0$ for any other gain.
  • With limited torque, bang-bang stops the body in finite time, with a bound $\sqrt{2I_{\max}T_0}/u_{\max}$ that is easy to compute at design time.
  • B-dot detumbles with a magnetometer and three coils, and stops at about twice the orbital rate because it damps motion relative to a field that itself rotates.
