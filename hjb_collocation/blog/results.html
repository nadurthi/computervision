6 · Sparse vs least-degree interpolation

Before any control problem, the paper compares the two ways of choosing a basis on a plain interpolation task: Gauss–Legendre points, a dictionary of the 28 monomials of degree ≤ 6, and two target functions,

$$ p_1(\mathbf x)=\frac{3x_1^3+2x_1^2x_2+2x_1x_2^2+5x_2^3+8x_1^6+16x_1^3x_2^3+8x_2^6}{10^3}\ \text{ on }[-5,5]^2, $$ $$ p_2(\mathbf x)=\frac{x_1^4\sin x_1+10x_2^4\cos^2x_2+x_1^8x_2+100x_2^6}{100}\ \text{ on }[-4,4]^2 . $$

6.1 Polynomial target (rerun)

With 16 points (4 × 4) and a degree-6 polynomial target, the ℓ1 problem \(\min\|\mathbf c\|_1\) s.t. \(A\mathbf c=\mathbf V\) returns exactly the 7 monomials of \(p_1\) with the right coefficients, even though 28 candidates and only 16 equations leave a 12-dimensional family of interpolants. The least-degree interpolant fills the same 16 equations with low-degree terms and misses the function between the points.

6.2 Non-polynomial target (paper)

\(p_2\) contains a degree-9 term \(x_1^8x_2\) that is not in the dictionary, so no interpolant can be exact. With 9 points the paper reports an RMSE of 98.99 for the ℓ1 interpolant against 350.08 for least degree; with 16 points, 50.84 against 330.86. The ℓ1 fit puts its error near the corners, while the least-degree fit is wrong across the whole domain.

7 · Example 2: spin stabilization (reproduces exactly)

Detumbling a rigid body with principal inertias \(I=\operatorname{diag}(14,10,8)\) kg·m², using Euler's equations:

$$ I_1\dot\omega_1+(I_3-I_2)\omega_2\omega_3=u_1,\quad I_2\dot\omega_2+(I_1-I_3)\omega_3\omega_1=u_2,\quad I_3\dot\omega_3+(I_2-I_1)\omega_1\omega_2=u_3, $$

with \(l=q\|\boldsymbol\omega\|^2\), \(R=rI\), domain \([-1,1]^3\), the 59 CUT8 points, and the full degree-6 dictionary (84 monomials). There are two cases: high state cost (\(q=50,r=3,\mathbf u_0=-\boldsymbol\omega\)) and high control cost (\(q=5,r=30,\mathbf u_0=-5\boldsymbol\omega\)).

For both cases, policy iteration converges in 7–8 steps, and the ℓ1 selection keeps 3 of the 84 monomials:

$$ V(\boldsymbol\omega)=171.464\,\omega_1^2+122.474\,\omega_2^2+97.980\,\omega_3^2 , $$

the same polynomial the paper reports. It is exactly the analytic solution. Try \(V=\alpha\sum_iI_i\omega_i^2\), i.e. \(2\alpha\times\) kinetic energy. The gyroscopic terms cancel, \(\sum_i I_i\omega_i\dot\omega_i\big|_{\mathbf u=0}=0\), and \(\mathbf g^\top V_{\boldsymbol\omega}=2\alpha\boldsymbol\omega\), so the HJB reduces to

$$ q\|\boldsymbol\omega\|^2-\frac{\alpha^2}{r}\|\boldsymbol\omega\|^2=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\alpha=\sqrt{qr}=\sqrt{150}=12.2474, \qquad \mathbf u^*=-\frac{\alpha}{r}\,\boldsymbol\omega . $$

Both cases have \(qr=150\), so they share one value function and differ only in the gain \(\alpha/r\). This is the classical Debs–Athans result.

8 · Example 1: Van der Pol oscillator

The forced Van der Pol oscillator on the domain \([-3,3]^2\):

$$ \dot x_1=x_2,\qquad \dot x_2=-x_1+(1-x_1^2)\,x_2+u,\qquad l(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^\top Q\mathbf x,\quad Q=50I,\quad R=5 . $$

Policy iteration starts from the stabilizing \(u_0=-1.2(x_1+x_2)\). The collocation set is the 21 CUT8 points scaled to the domain plus 8 boundary points (Fig. 2, left), 29 points in all, and the dictionary holds the 36 monomials of degree ≤ 7. The controller converges within 20 policy iterations, and the ℓ1 selection keeps 14 of the 36 monomials:

$$ \begin{aligned}V(\mathbf x)&=33.926x_1^2+3.047x_1x_2+27.881x_2^2+4.349x_1^4+8.09x_1^3x_2-1.735x_1^2x_2^2-1.216x_1x_2^3-1.265x_2^4\\&\quad-0.731x_1^5x_2-0.208x_1^4x_2^2-0.043x_1^3x_2^3+0.049x_1^2x_2^4+0.106x_1x_2^5+0.076x_2^6 .\end{aligned} $$

Only even-degree terms survive, as they should for odd dynamics and an even cost: \(V(-\mathbf x)=V(\mathbf x)\). The optimal law \(u^*=-\tfrac1{2R}\,\partial V/\partial x_2\) overpowers the open-loop limit cycle and drives every trajectory to the origin along a common slow manifold, at a much lower cost than \(u_0\).

8.1 Least-degree basis instead of ℓ1

Running the same policy iteration with the least-degree basis (de Boor–Ron) on the same 29 points converges poorly. Trajectories reach the origin slowly, starts near the top and bottom of the domain go unstable (red markers), and the level sets of \(V\) are no longer closed there, so \(\dot V<0\) fails over part of \(\Omega\). The least-degree basis is chosen to condition the Vandermonde matrix, not to represent the value function, and it shows. My Python rerun agrees on this point: with a least-degree basis, 16 of 48 test starts diverge.

About the rerun of this example. The Python port reproduces the spin example and interpolation Example 1 exactly, so the figures above for this example are the paper's. Rerunning Van der Pol from the setup as written lands on a different 14-term polynomial. The reason is that the problem gives the ℓ1 step a lot of freedom. The CUT set is symmetric under \(\mathbf x\to-\mathbf x\) and the dynamics are odd, so the 29 collocation rows contain only 14 independent equations for the 15 even monomials. Which single monomial is dropped decides the answer, and small differences in setup change that choice.
