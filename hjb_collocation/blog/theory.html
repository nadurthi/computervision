1 · The problem: infinite-horizon optimal feedback

For a control-affine system we want a feedback law \(\mathbf u=\mathbf k(\mathbf x)\), not an open-loop trajectory:

$$ \min_{\mathbf u(\cdot)}\; V(\mathbf x_0)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(l(\mathbf x)+\mathbf u^\top R\,\mathbf u\Big)\,dt \qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad \dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf f(\mathbf x)+\mathbf g(\mathbf x)\,\mathbf u,\quad \mathbf x_0\in\Omega , $$

with \(\mathbf f(\mathbf 0)=\mathbf 0\), \(l\) positive definite and \(R\succ0\). Minimizing the Hamiltonian \(H=l+\mathbf u^\top R\mathbf u+V_{\mathbf x}^\top(\mathbf f+\mathbf g\mathbf u)\) over \(\mathbf u\) gives the control in terms of the value-function gradient,

$$ \mathbf u^*(\mathbf x)=-\tfrac12 R^{-1}\mathbf g(\mathbf x)^\top \frac{\partial V}{\partial \mathbf x}, $$

and substituting back gives the stationary Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation, with \(V(\mathbf 0)=0\):

$$ l(\mathbf x)+\frac{\partial V}{\partial \mathbf x}^{\!\top}\mathbf f(\mathbf x)-\frac14\,\frac{\partial V}{\partial \mathbf x}^{\!\top}\mathbf g(\mathbf x)R^{-1}\mathbf g(\mathbf x)^\top\frac{\partial V}{\partial \mathbf x}=0 . $$

Solve this PDE over \(\Omega\) and you have the optimal controller for every initial state at once. For a linear system with \(l=\mathbf x^\top Q\mathbf x\) it collapses to the algebraic Riccati equation and \(V=\mathbf x^\top P\mathbf x\). For anything nonlinear, two things make it hard: it is quadratic in \(V_{\mathbf x}\), and it lives in the full state space.

2 · Policy iteration: one linear PDE at a time

Kleinman showed that the Riccati equation can be solved as a sequence of Lyapunov equations. Saridis and Lee, and later Beard, Saridis and Wen, extended the idea to the nonlinear HJB. Fix a stabilizing (admissible) policy \(\mathbf u_{(k)}\). Its cost-to-go satisfies the generalized HJB (GHJB), which is linear in \(V\):

$$ \textit{Step 1:}\quad \frac{\partial V^{(k)}}{\partial\mathbf x}^{\!\top}\Big[\mathbf f(\mathbf x)+\mathbf g(\mathbf x)\mathbf u_{(k)}(\mathbf x)\Big]+l(\mathbf x)+\mathbf u_{(k)}^\top R\,\mathbf u_{(k)}=0 $$ $$ \textit{Step 2:}\quad \mathbf u_{(k+1)}(\mathbf x)=-\tfrac12R^{-1}\mathbf g(\mathbf x)^\top\frac{\partial V^{(k)}}{\partial\mathbf x} $$

If each GHJB is solved exactly, every new policy is admissible and no worse than the last, and \(V^{(k)}\) decreases monotonically to the HJB solution. That guarantee is about the exact GHJB. Once the GHJB is solved approximately, as any numerical method must, the guarantee is only as good as the approximation, so the choice of points and basis below matters.

3 · Weighted residuals: Galerkin, least squares, collocation

Write the value function as a linear combination of basis functions with \(\phi_i(\mathbf 0)=0\):

$$ V(\mathbf x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_i\,\phi_i(\mathbf x)=\mathbf c^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x),\qquad \frac{\partial V}{\partial\mathbf x}^{\!\top}=\mathbf c^\top J(\mathbf x),\quad J_{ij}=\frac{\partial\phi_i}{\partial x_j}. $$

The GHJB residual is then linear in \(\mathbf c\):

$$ e(\mathbf x)=\mathbf c^\top J(\mathbf x)\big[\mathbf f+\mathbf g\mathbf u_{(k)}\big]+l(\mathbf x)+\mathbf u_{(k)}^\top R\,\mathbf u_{(k)} . $$

The method of weighted residuals sets \(\int_\Omega\Psi_j(\mathbf x)\,e(\mathbf x)\,d\mathbf x=0\) for \(m\) test functions \(\Psi_j\):

MethodTest function \(\Psi_j\)Cost per GHJB
Galerkin (Beard et al.)\(\phi_j\)\(O(m^2)\) integrals over \(\Omega\subset\mathbb R^n\)
Least squares\(\partial e/\partial c_j\)\(O(m^2)\) integrals over \(\Omega\)
Collocation\(\delta(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_j)\)no integrals: \(e(\mathbf x_j)=0\) at \(N\) points

Collocation turns the GHJB into a linear system \(D\mathbf c=F\), one row per point:

$$ \mathbf d_i^\top=\Big(J(\mathbf x_i)\big[\mathbf f(\mathbf x_i)+\mathbf g(\mathbf x_i)\mathbf u_{(k)}(\mathbf x_i)\big]\Big)^\top,\qquad F_i=-l(\mathbf x_i)-\mathbf u_{(k)}(\mathbf x_i)^\top R\,\mathbf u_{(k)}(\mathbf x_i). $$

That leaves two design choices, and the paper's contribution is one answer to each: where to put the \(N\) points (§4), and which \(m\) basis functions to use (§5).

4 · Collocation points: the Conjugate Unscented Transformation

Gaussian quadrature is the natural choice in 1-D, but its \(n\)-D version is a tensor product: 5 points per axis in 6-D is \(5^6=15{,}625\) points. Sparse (Smolyak) grids cut that down but bring negative weights. The Conjugate Unscented Transformation (CUT) builds symmetric point sets directly in \(\mathbb R^n\), placed on three families of axes:

  • principal axes \(\sigma_i=\pm\mathbf e_k\): \(2n\) points;
  • \(p\)-th conjugate axes \(c^p\): every sign and combination of \(p\) principal axes, \(2^p\binom np\) points, e.g. \((\pm1,\pm1,0,\dots)\);
  • scaled conjugate axes \(s^p(h)\): the same, with one coordinate scaled by \(h\), \(n2^p\binom np\) points, e.g. \((\pm h,\pm1,0,\dots)\).

Each family gets one radius \(r\) and one weight \(w\). Symmetry makes every odd moment vanish automatically, so only the even moment equations are solved for the \((r,w)\) pairs. For example, CUT6 (exact to 7th order, \(n\le6\)) uses the principal axes, the \(n\)-th conjugate axes and the 2nd conjugate axes, for \(N=2n^2+2^n+1\) points, with

$$ w_1=\frac{8-n}{r_1^6},\qquad w_2=\frac{1}{2^nr_2^6},\qquad w_3=\frac{1}{2r_3^6}, $$

and \(a_i=1/r_i^2\) solving three small polynomial equations.

5 · Picking the basis: iterative weighted ℓ1

In 1-D, \(N\) points pick out a unique interpolating polynomial of degree \(N-1\). In \(n\)-D they don't. There are many monomials of each degree, and which subset is well posed depends on where the points are. The classical answer is de Boor and Ron's least interpolation: Gaussian elimination on the Vandermonde matrix, column by column in order of degree. The paper replaces it with a sparse-approximation problem over an over-complete dictionary:

$$ P_3:\quad \min_{\mathbf c}\ \|W\mathbf c\|_1\quad\text{s.t.}\quad D\mathbf c=F,\qquad A\mathbf c\ge \mathbf 0,\qquad \mathbf c^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf 0)=0,\qquad \mathbf c_{\mathcal C}=\mathbf 0 . $$

\(A_{ij}=\phi_j(\mathbf x_i)\), so \(A\mathbf c\ge0\) asks for \(V\ge0\) at the points. The weights \(W=\operatorname{diag}(w_j)\) grow with total degree, so high-degree terms cost more. \(\mathcal C\) is a set of coefficients forced to zero. Algorithm 1 grows \(\mathcal C\) one index at a time, always adding the smallest surviving coefficient, until only \(N\) terms are left.

Algorithm 1  (iterative ℓ1, c* = Opt(m, N)(D, F, A))
  C ← ∅
  for i = 0 … m − N:
      c* ← argmin ‖W c‖₁  s.t.  D c = F,  A c ≥ 0,  φ(0)ᵀc = 0,  c_C = 0
      C  ← C ∪ { argmin_{j ∉ C} |c*_j| }

Algorithm 2  (collocation policy iteration)
  A ← [φ_j(x_i)]
  for k = 0 … K:
      build D, F from u_(k)          # one GHJB collocation row per point
      c ← Opt(m, N)(D, F, A)
      u_(k+1)(x) ← −½ R⁻¹ g(x)ᵀ J(x)ᵀ c

Both norms are piecewise linear, so each step is a linear program. The rerun writes it with split variables \(\mathbf c=\mathbf p-\mathbf q\), \(\mathbf p,\mathbf q\ge0\), cost \(\mathbf w^\top(\mathbf p+\mathbf q)\), and solves it with HiGHS; the original used CVX. One practical point the rerun turned up: an ℓ1 optimum sits at a vertex of the feasible polytope, so it usually has no more nonzeros than equality constraints to begin with. In the examples below, the first LP already returns fewer than \(N\) nonzero coefficients, and the sequential zeroing loop never has to remove anything.
