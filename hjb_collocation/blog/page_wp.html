Sparse collocation for the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation

An optimal feedback law for a nonlinear system is the gradient of a value function $V(\mathbf x)$ that solves the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman PDE over the whole state space. That PDE hits the curse of dimensionality twice: in the number of points needed to discretize it, and in the number of basis functions needed to represent $V$. In 2015–16 we attacked both at once (Adurthi, Singla & Majji, JGCD 2017). CUT points keep the collocation grid small, an iterative ℓ1 program picks a sparse polynomial basis out of an over-complete dictionary, and policy iteration turns the nonlinear PDE into a sequence of linear ones. This page derives the method, then reruns every example in Python, checked against the original MATLAB code and against the true optimum from Pontryagin's principle.

1 · The problem: infinite-horizon optimal feedback

For a control-affine system we want a feedback law $\mathbf u=\mathbf k(\mathbf x)$, not an open-loop trajectory:

$$ \min_{\mathbf u(\cdot)}\; V(\mathbf x_0)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(l(\mathbf x)+\mathbf u^\top R\,\mathbf u\Big)\,dt \qquad\text{s.t.}\qquad \dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf f(\mathbf x)+\mathbf g(\mathbf x)\,\mathbf u,\quad \mathbf x_0\in\Omega , $$

with $\mathbf f(\mathbf 0)=\mathbf 0$, $l$ positive definite and $R\succ0$. Minimizing the Hamiltonian $H=l+\mathbf u^\top R\mathbf u+V_{\mathbf x}^\top(\mathbf f+\mathbf g\mathbf u)$ over $\mathbf u$ gives the control in terms of the value-function gradient,

$$ \mathbf u^*(\mathbf x)=-\tfrac12 R^{-1}\mathbf g(\mathbf x)^\top \frac{\partial V}{\partial \mathbf x}, $$

and substituting back gives the stationary Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation, with $V(\mathbf 0)=0$:

$$ l(\mathbf x)+\frac{\partial V}{\partial \mathbf x}^{\!\top}\mathbf f(\mathbf x)-\frac14\,\frac{\partial V}{\partial \mathbf x}^{\!\top}\mathbf g(\mathbf x)R^{-1}\mathbf g(\mathbf x)^\top\frac{\partial V}{\partial \mathbf x}=0 . $$

Solve this PDE over $\Omega$ and you have the optimal controller for every initial state at once. For a linear system with $l=\mathbf x^\top Q\mathbf x$ it collapses to the algebraic Riccati equation and $V=\mathbf x^\top P\mathbf x$. For anything nonlinear, two things make it hard: it is quadratic in $V_{\mathbf x}$, and it lives in the full state space.

2 · Policy iteration: one linear PDE at a time

Kleinman showed that the Riccati equation can be solved as a sequence of Lyapunov equations. Saridis and Lee, and later Beard, Saridis and Wen, extended the idea to the nonlinear HJB. Fix a stabilizing (admissible) policy $\mathbf u_{(k)}$. Its cost-to-go satisfies the generalized HJB (GHJB), which is linear in $V$:

$$ \textit{Step 1:}\quad \frac{\partial V^{(k)}}{\partial\mathbf x}^{\!\top}\Big[\mathbf f(\mathbf x)+\mathbf g(\mathbf x)\mathbf u_{(k)}(\mathbf x)\Big]+l(\mathbf x)+\mathbf u_{(k)}^\top R\,\mathbf u_{(k)}=0 $$ $$ \textit{Step 2:}\quad \mathbf u_{(k+1)}(\mathbf x)=-\tfrac12R^{-1}\mathbf g(\mathbf x)^\top\frac{\partial V^{(k)}}{\partial\mathbf x} $$

If each GHJB is solved exactly, every new policy is admissible and no worse than the last, and $V^{(k)}$ decreases monotonically to the HJB solution. That guarantee is about the exact GHJB. Once the GHJB is solved approximately, as any numerical method must, the guarantee is only as good as the approximation, and §8 shows what that means in practice.

3 · Weighted residuals: Galerkin, least squares, collocation

Write the value function as a linear combination of basis functions with $\phi_i(\mathbf 0)=0$:

$$ V(\mathbf x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_i\,\phi_i(\mathbf x)=\mathbf c^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x),\qquad \frac{\partial V}{\partial\mathbf x}^{\!\top}=\mathbf c^\top J(\mathbf x),\quad J_{ij}=\frac{\partial\phi_i}{\partial x_j}. $$

The GHJB residual is then linear in $\mathbf c$:

$$ e(\mathbf x)=\mathbf c^\top J(\mathbf x)\big[\mathbf f+\mathbf g\mathbf u_{(k)}\big]+l(\mathbf x)+\mathbf u_{(k)}^\top R\,\mathbf u_{(k)} . $$

The method of weighted residuals sets $\int_\Omega\Psi_j(\mathbf x)\,e(\mathbf x)\,d\mathbf x=0$ for $m$ test functions $\Psi_j$:

MethodTest function $\Psi_j$Cost per GHJB
Galerkin (Beard et al.)$\phi_j$$O(m^2)$ integrals over $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^n$
Least squares$\partial e/\partial c_j$$O(m^2)$ integrals over $\Omega$
Collocation$\delta(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_j)$no integrals: $e(\mathbf x_j)=0$ at $N$ points

Collocation turns the GHJB into a linear system $D\mathbf c=F$, one row per point:

$$ \mathbf d_i^\top=\Big(J(\mathbf x_i)\big[\mathbf f(\mathbf x_i)+\mathbf g(\mathbf x_i)\mathbf u_{(k)}(\mathbf x_i)\big]\Big)^\top,\qquad F_i=-l(\mathbf x_i)-\mathbf u_{(k)}(\mathbf x_i)^\top R\,\mathbf u_{(k)}(\mathbf x_i). $$

That leaves two design choices, and the paper's contribution is one answer to each: where to put the $N$ points (§4), and which $m$ basis functions to use (§5).

4 · Collocation points: the Conjugate Unscented Transformation

Gaussian quadrature is the natural choice in 1-D, but its $n$-D version is a tensor product: 5 points per axis in 6-D is $5^6=15{,}625$ points. Sparse (Smolyak) grids cut that down but bring negative weights. The Conjugate Unscented Transformation (CUT) builds symmetric point sets directly in $\mathbb R^n$, placed on three families of axes:

  • principal axes $\sigma_i=\pm\mathbf e_k$: $2n$ points;
  • $p$-th conjugate axes $c^p$: every sign and combination of $p$ principal axes, $2^p\binom np$ points, e.g. $(\pm1,\pm1,0,\dots)$;
  • scaled conjugate axes $s^p(h)$: the same, with one coordinate scaled by $h$, $n2^p\binom np$ points, e.g. $(\pm h,\pm1,0,\dots)$.

Each family gets one radius $r$ and one weight $w$. Symmetry makes every odd moment vanish automatically, so only the even moment equations are solved for the $(r,w)$ pairs. For example, CUT6 (exact to 7th order, $n\le6$) uses the principal axes, the $n$-th conjugate axes and the 2nd conjugate axes, for $N=2n^2+2^n+1$ points, with

$$ w_1=\frac{8-n}{r_1^6},\qquad w_2=\frac{1}{2^nr_2^6},\qquad w_3=\frac{1}{2r_3^6}, $$

and $a_i=1/r_i^2$ solving three small polynomial equations.

The CUT8 rules used here are exact for all moments up to 9th order:

The last row is the key to the next section. With CUT points, the dictionary of candidate monomials grows faster than the number of points, so $D\mathbf c=F$ is under-determined: there are infinitely many value functions that satisfy the GHJB exactly at every point.

5 · Picking the basis: iterative weighted ℓ1

In 1-D, $N$ points pick out a unique interpolating polynomial of degree $N-1$. In $n$-D they don't. There are many monomials of each degree, and which subset is well posed depends on where the points are. The classical answer is de Boor and Ron's least interpolation: Gaussian elimination on the Vandermonde matrix, column by column in order of degree. The paper replaces it with a sparse-approximation problem over an over-complete dictionary:

$$ P_3:\quad \min_{\mathbf c}\ \|W\mathbf c\|_1\quad\text{s.t.}\quad D\mathbf c=F,\qquad A\mathbf c\ge \mathbf 0,\qquad \mathbf c^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf 0)=0,\qquad \mathbf c_{\mathcal C}=\mathbf 0 . $$

$A_{ij}=\phi_j(\mathbf x_i)$, so $A\mathbf c\ge0$ asks for $V\ge0$ at the points. The weights $W=\operatorname{diag}(w_j)$ grow with total degree, so high-degree terms cost more. $\mathcal C$ is a set of coefficients forced to zero. Algorithm 1 grows $\mathcal C$ one index at a time, always adding the smallest surviving coefficient, until only $N$ terms are left.

Algorithm 1  (iterative ℓ1, c* = Opt(m, N)(D, F, A))
  C ← ∅
  for i = 0 … m − N:
      c* ← argmin ‖W c‖₁  s.t.  D c = F,  A c ≥ 0,  φ(0)ᵀc = 0,  c_C = 0
      C  ← C ∪ { argmin_{j ∉ C} |c*_j| }

Algorithm 2  (collocation policy iteration)
  A ← [φ_j(x_i)]
  for k = 0 … K:
      build D, F from u_(k)          # one GHJB collocation row per point
      c ← Opt(m, N)(D, F, A)
      u_(k+1)(x) ← −½ R⁻¹ g(x)ᵀ J(x)ᵀ c

Both norms are piecewise linear, so each step is a linear program. The rerun writes it with split variables $\mathbf c=\mathbf p-\mathbf q$, $\mathbf p,\mathbf q\ge0$, cost $\mathbf w^\top(\mathbf p+\mathbf q)$, and solves it with HiGHS; the original used CVX. One practical point the rerun turned up: an ℓ1 optimum sits at a vertex of the feasible polytope, so it usually has no more nonzeros than equality constraints to begin with. In every example below, the first LP already returns fewer than $N$ nonzero coefficients, and the sequential zeroing loop never has to remove anything.

7 · Example 2: spin stabilization (reproduces exactly)

Detumbling a rigid body with principal inertias $I=\operatorname{diag}(14,10,8)$ kg·m², using Euler's equations:

$$ I_1\dot\omega_1+(I_3-I_2)\omega_2\omega_3=u_1,\quad I_2\dot\omega_2+(I_1-I_3)\omega_3\omega_1=u_2,\quad I_3\dot\omega_3+(I_2-I_1)\omega_1\omega_2=u_3, $$

with $l=q\|\boldsymbol\omega\|^2$, $R=rI$, domain $[-1,1]^3$, the 59 CUT8 points, and the full degree-6 dictionary (84 monomials). There are two cases: high state cost ($q=50,r=3,\mathbf u_0=-\boldsymbol\omega$) and high control cost ($q=5,r=30,\mathbf u_0=-5\boldsymbol\omega$).

For both cases, policy iteration converges in 7–8 steps, and the ℓ1 selection keeps 3 of the 84 monomials:

$$ V(\boldsymbol\omega)=171.464\,\omega_1^2+122.474\,\omega_2^2+97.980\,\omega_3^2 , $$

the same polynomial the paper reports. It is exactly the analytic solution. Try $V=\alpha\sum_iI_i\omega_i^2$, i.e. $2\alpha\times$ kinetic energy. The gyroscopic terms cancel, $\sum_i I_i\omega_i\dot\omega_i\big|_{\mathbf u=0}=0$, and $\mathbf g^\top V_{\boldsymbol\omega}=2\alpha\boldsymbol\omega$, so the HJB reduces to

$$ q\|\boldsymbol\omega\|^2-\frac{\alpha^2}{r}\|\boldsymbol\omega\|^2=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\alpha=\sqrt{qr}=\sqrt{150}=12.2474, \qquad \mathbf u^*=-\frac{\alpha}{r}\,\boldsymbol\omega . $$

Both cases have $qr=150$, so they share one value function and differ only in the gain $\alpha/r$. This is the classical Debs–Athans result.

The ℓ1 objective is also doing real work here. 59 equations with 84 unknowns leave a 25-dimensional family of exact interpolants, and the sparsest one happens to be the true solution.

8 · Example 1: Van der Pol (does not reproduce)

The forced Van der Pol oscillator, with $Q=50I$, $R=5$, $\mathbf u_0=-1.2(x_1+x_2)$ and the domain $[-3,3]^2$:

$$ \dot x_1=x_2,\qquad \dot x_2=-x_1+(1-x_1^2)\,x_2+u . $$

The paper uses the 21 CUT8 points plus the 8 boundary points (29 in all) and a degree-7 dictionary (36 monomials). It reports a 14-term value function after at most 20 policy iterations:

8.1 Why the rerun lands somewhere else

Two checks narrow it down.

The published V is not a solution of the described collocation problem. A converged policy-iteration solution satisfies its own GHJB exactly at every collocation point. Plugging the published coefficients into $D(\mathbf u_{\text{paper}})\mathbf c=F(\mathbf u_{\text{paper}})$ at the 29 points described gives a residual RMS of 317, against a right-hand side of RMS 879. So the published run differed from the description in some respect, such as the point set, scaling or iteration count. The repository has saved workspaces only for earlier Gauss–Legendre runs, not for this one. Rounding of the printed coefficients can't explain it either: even worst-case rounding (±0.0005 on each, ±0.005 on the two-decimal $x_1^3x_2$) moves the residual by at most 28, under a tenth of it.

The described problem barely constrains V. The CUT set is symmetric under $\mathbf x\to-\mathbf x$, and the Van der Pol vector field is odd. For an even $V$, the collocation rows at $\mathbf x_i$ and $-\mathbf x_i$ are identical, and the row at the origin is zero. So the 29 equations have rank 28 over all 36 columns but only 14 over the 15 even monomials, and every iteration leaves exactly one even monomial to drop. The ℓ1 objective chooses which one, and the choice matters a lot:

Dropping $x_1^3x_2$ or $x_1^5x_2$ gives a controller within 21 % of optimal at the median, about as good as the published one. Dropping $x_2^2$ or $x_2^4$ gives one that diverges from some starts. The ℓ1 objective has no way to tell these apart, because it scores coefficient size, not closed-loop cost. On this problem the method's outcome is set by an essentially arbitrary tie-break. Adding a second, inner ring of CUT points (49 points) did not help either: at degree 10 and 12 the policies were worse still.

The general lesson. Policy iteration's monotone-improvement guarantee assumes each GHJB is solved exactly. With $N$ collocation points and $m>N$ unknowns, "exact at the points" leaves an $(m-\text{rank}\,D)$-dimensional family of value functions, and the regularizer picks one. When the true $V$ is sparse in the dictionary, as in the spin example, the ℓ1 pick is the right one. When it isn't, the pick can be far from the policy's real cost-to-go, and the next policy inherits that error. The quantity worth monitoring is not convergence of $\mathbf c$ (the rerun's coefficients stop changing at the 10⁻⁹ level) but the gap between $V(\mathbf x_0)$ and the simulated closed-loop cost.

10 · Checking the port against the MATLAB code

A reproduction that disagrees with a paper is only useful if the reproduction is right. The HJB repository holds the original MATLAB scripts and several saved workspaces from earlier development runs (Gauss–Legendre points, degree-6 dictionary). One of them, HJBcollSol_2D_vanderpoll_c1_lowcontrol.mat ($Q=25I$, $R=3$), ends with CVX status Solved. It stores the collocation points, the final $D$, $F$ and constraint matrices, and the coefficients from all 40 policy iterations. Replaying it in Python:

The workspace with the paper's exact cost weights ($Q=50I$, $R=5$) is not usable as a reference, because its last CVX solve reports Failed and its stored coefficients violate their own equality constraints. That earlier Gauss–Legendre setup also never converged (its coefficients were still moving at iteration 40), which is presumably why the paper moved to CUT points.

The Python LP differs from the MATLAB code in two harmless ways. It uses HiGHS with split variables instead of CVX, and it skips Algorithm 1's zeroing loop when the first ℓ1 solution already has at most $N$ nonzeros. Zeroing coefficients that are already zero cannot change an LP optimum, so the result is identical.

11 · What to take from it

  • The two ideas work where their assumptions hold. CUT points make collocation in 6-D affordable (about a thousand points instead of 15,625). The ℓ1 program finds the true value function exactly when that function is sparse in the dictionary, as in the spin example.
  • Count independent equations, not points. Symmetric point sets combined with odd or even dynamics produce duplicate rows. In the Van der Pol case, 29 points gave 14 equations. Check $\operatorname{rank}D$ on the columns that can actually be nonzero.
  • Validate closed-loop, not coefficient convergence. Every run here converged. The useful diagnostics are $V(\mathbf x_0)$ against the simulated cost of the policy it defines, the HJB residual off the collocation points, and, in low dimension, the Pontryagin optimum from a few starting states.
  • ℓ1 is a tie-breaker, not a physics prior. When the collocation equations leave freedom, ℓ1 picks the smallest coefficients, not the best controller. A natural fix is to add rows that the policy actually needs, such as more points along the closed-loop flow, or a least-squares term on extra test points.
