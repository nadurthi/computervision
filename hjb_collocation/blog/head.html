Sparse collocation for the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation

An optimal feedback law for a nonlinear system is the gradient of a value function \(V(\mathbf x)\) that solves the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman PDE over the whole state space. That PDE hits the curse of dimensionality twice: in the number of points needed to discretize it, and in the number of basis functions needed to represent \(V\). In 2015–16 we attacked both at once (Adurthi, Singla & Majji, JGCD 2017). CUT points keep the collocation grid small, an iterative ℓ1 program picks a sparse polynomial basis out of an over-complete dictionary, and policy iteration turns the nonlinear PDE into a sequence of linear ones. This page derives the method, then reruns every example in Python, checked against the original MATLAB code and against the true optimum from Pontryagin's principle.

What the rerun found.
  • Spin stabilization reproduces exactly. From 84 candidate monomials, the ℓ1 selection keeps exactly the three terms of the analytic solution \(V=\sqrt{qr}\sum I_i\omega_i^2\), with coefficients correct to 10⁻¹⁴.
  • The interpolation benchmark reproduces on the polynomial test (the least-degree error matches the paper's 106.1224 exactly). On the non-polynomial test the ranking holds, but the RMSE values come out larger than the published ones.
  • Van der Pol does not reproduce. The published value function is a good controller (13 % above the true optimum at the median), but it doesn't satisfy the collocation equations as described. Rerunning the described setup gives a different 14-term \(V\) whose policy is barely better than the initial one. The cause is structural: symmetric points and odd dynamics leave only 14 independent equations, and which single monomial ℓ1 drops swings the result from 1.2× optimal to unstable.
