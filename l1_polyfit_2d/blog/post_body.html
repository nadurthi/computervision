Fitting a density from 21 points: CUT points, Gauss–Hermite grids and an L1 linear program

Suppose you can only evaluate a 2D probability density at a handful of points, because each evaluation means integrating a trajectory or running a simulation. You want a closed-form approximation of the whole surface: something you can integrate, sample from and evaluate anywhere. This page fits \(f(\mathbf x)\approx \exp\big(p(\mathbf x)\big)\) with a polynomial \(p\) found by one linear program with an L1 penalty, and compares three ways of choosing where to evaluate \(f\): a uniform grid, a Gauss–Hermite tensor grid and CUT-8 points. With 21 points, CUT-8 gets 4.1 % error. The two 25-point grids get 14–15 %. The reason turns out to be linear algebra, not quadrature accuracy.

The method is a Python port of get_interp_pdf_0I_2D_gobackget_L1.m from my method-of-characteristics filtering code (nadurthi/moc2), where the propagated density is known only at the points that were propagated. The port uses no MATLAB or CVX: the problem is written out as an explicit LP and solved with SciPy's HiGHS. Code, point sets and every figure on this page: CODE_LINK.

1 · The model: a polynomial in log space

The approximation is an exponential family with polynomial sufficient statistics:

$$ f(\mathbf x)\;\approx\;\hat f(\mathbf x)=\exp\!\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_i\,\phi_i(\mathbf x)\Big),\qquad \phi_i(x,y)=x^{a_i}y^{b_i},\quad a_i+b_i\le N_m . $$

Working in log space has three advantages. \(\hat f\) is positive everywhere by construction. A Gaussian is exactly a degree-2 member of the family, so the model starts from the right shape and higher degrees add skew, curvature and heavy tails. And this is the maximum-entropy form for matching the moments \(\mathbb E[\phi_i]\), which is where the \(\lambda\) name comes from. With a total-degree basis in \(d\) dimensions there are

$$ n=\binom{d+N_m}{N_m}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad n=28 \ \text{ for } d=2,\ N_m=6 $$

unknowns. Evaluating the basis at \(N\) points gives the design matrix \(A\in\mathbb R^{N\times n}\), \(A_{ji}=\phi_i(\mathbf x_j)\), and the data are \(b_j=\log f(\mathbf x_j)\). With 21–25 points and 28 unknowns, the problem is underdetermined. The point set and the regularizer decide which of the infinitely many interpolants you get.

The test surface. A Student-t banana (\(\nu=4\)): $$\log f = -\tfrac{\nu+1}{2}\log\!\Big(1+\tfrac{q}{\nu}\Big),\qquad q=\Big(\tfrac{x}{0.35}\Big)^2+\Big(\tfrac{y-1.6x^2+0.35}{0.22}\Big)^2 .$$ It has the curved shape of the usual Gaussian banana, but its log is not a polynomial, so no degree can represent it exactly. The Gaussian banana's log is exactly a degree-4 polynomial and makes a misleadingly easy test (§7).

2 · Three ways to choose 21–25 points

All three sets are defined for a standard normal, \(\mathbf x\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\), and then scaled into the fitting box (§2.4). Figure 2 shows them in their own coordinates, with marker area proportional to quadrature weight.

2.1 Uniform grid

A 5 × 5 tensor grid of equally spaced nodes, with equal weights. It has no quadrature optimality at all and serves as the baseline.

2.2 Gauss–Hermite

The 5-node Gauss–Hermite rule (probabilists' version) places nodes at the roots of the Hermite polynomial \(He_5\), and is exact for every 1D polynomial up to degree \(2\cdot5-1=9\):

$$ \int g(x)\,\mathcal N(x;0,1)\,dx=\sum_{k=1}^{5}w_k\,g(x_k)\quad\text{for }\deg g\le 9,\qquad x_k\in\{0,\pm1.356,\pm2.857\},\ w_k\in\{0.533,0.222,0.011\}. $$

The 2D tensor product uses all 25 pairs \((x_k,x_l)\) with weights \(w_kw_l\). It is exact for \(x^iy^j\) whenever \(i\le 9\) and \(j\le 9\), a square in the \((i,j)\) plane. Its cost is \(5^d\) points, which grows exponentially with dimension.

2.3 CUT-8: Conjugate Unscented Transformation

CUT rules (Adurthi, Singla & Singh) keep the unscented transform's idea of a few symmetric, deterministic points, but choose their positions to match moments up to a much higher order. The points lie on symmetric orbits: the center, the principal axes, the diagonals and "conjugate" axes such as \((\pm a,\pm b)\) and \((\pm b,\pm a)\). The radii and weights are solved from the moment equations. The 2D CUT-8 rule used here has 21 points:

The weights sum to 1. Symmetry makes every odd moment vanish automatically, so only even moments need matching. Figure 3 checks both rules against the exact Gaussian moments \(\mathbb E[x^i]\,\mathbb E[y^j]\), with \(\mathbb E[x^{2k}]=(2k-1)!!\).

That difference in shape matters here. The polynomial basis \(\{x^ay^b: a+b\le N_m\}\) is a total-degree triangle, and CUT is designed for exactly that triangle. A tensor grid spends points on moments like \(x^9y^9\) (total degree 18) that a total-degree basis never uses. This is the usual efficiency argument for CUT and sparse rules. But it is not the main reason for the result below. §5 has the main reason.

2.4 Scaling into the fitting box

The MATLAB code works on a box of half-width 1 and scales each point set so its largest coordinate is 0.85. A shared scaling like that is not neutral. CUT-8's outermost points are at 3.4σ in one coordinate, so the rest of the set is pulled inward much more than Gauss–Hermite's (whose largest node is 2.86).

CUT-8 therefore samples the high-curvature middle of the box more densely. The grids still have a point closer to the peak itself (0.05 and 0.08 away, against 0.17 for CUT-8), so "CUT has a point on the peak" is not the explanation either.

3 · The optimization: one linear program

3.1 The problem

Sort the samples by value and split them by a log cutoff \(c=-9\). Points with \(b_j\ge c\) are fitted with slack. Points with \(b_j<c\) only have to stay low. Guard points \(\mathbf g_l\) outside \([-1.5,1.5]^2\) keep \(p\) below a Gaussian so that \(\exp(p)\) cannot blow up away from the data:

$$ \begin{aligned} \min_{\boldsymbol\lambda,\,\mathbf t}\quad & \frac{C}{n}\,\|\boldsymbol\lambda\|_1 \;+\; 10\cdot\operatorname{norm\_largest}(\mathbf t,\,k) \\ \text{s.t.}\quad & A_{\text{eq}}\boldsymbol\lambda = \mathbf b_{\text{eq}}+\mathbf t \\ & A_{\text{ineq}}\boldsymbol\lambda \le c\,\mathbf 1 \\ & A_{\text{out}}\boldsymbol\lambda \le \log\mathcal N(\mathbf g_l;0,I) \end{aligned} \qquad k=\lfloor 0.75\,n\rfloor . $$

\(\operatorname{norm\_largest}(\mathbf t,k)\) is the sum of the \(k\) largest \(|t_j|\). It sits between the \(\ell_\infty\) norm (\(k=1\)) and the \(\ell_1\) norm (\(k=N\)). The first term pushes coefficients to zero, so it acts as a sparsity prior on the polynomial.

3.2 Writing it as an LP

Both norms are piecewise linear, so the whole problem is an LP. Split \(|\lambda_i|\le s_i\). For the k-largest sum, use the dual form \(\operatorname{norm\_largest}(\mathbf t,k)=\min_{\sigma,\mathbf u}\{k\sigma+\sum_j u_j : u_j\ge |t_j|-\sigma,\ u_j\ge 0\}\). The variables are \(\mathbf z=[\boldsymbol\lambda,\mathbf s,\mathbf t,\mathbf u,\sigma]\):

$$ \min\ \frac{C}{n}\mathbf 1^\top\mathbf s+10\big(k\sigma+\mathbf 1^\top\mathbf u\big) \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \pm\boldsymbol\lambda-\mathbf s\le 0,\quad \pm\mathbf t-\mathbf u-\sigma\mathbf 1\le 0,\quad A_{\text{eq}}\boldsymbol\lambda-\mathbf t=\mathbf b_{\text{eq}},\quad \mathbf s,\mathbf u,\sigma\ge0 $$

plus the two inequality blocks on \(\boldsymbol\lambda\). For CUT-8 at degree 6 that is 99 variables, 21 equalities and about 400 inequalities (most of them guard points). HiGHS solves it in milliseconds.

# z = [lam (n), s (n), t (m), u (m), sigma (1)]
c = np.zeros(2*n + 2*m + 1)
c[n:2*n] = C / n                 # C/n * sum(s)      -> L1 on lambda
c[2*n+m:2*n+2*m] = 10.0          # 10 * sum(u)
c[-1] = 10.0 * k                 # 10 * k * sigma    -> norm_largest(t, k)
A_ub = vstack([[ I_n, -I_n, 0, 0, 0],          #  lam - s <= 0
               [-I_n, -I_n, 0, 0, 0],          # -lam - s <= 0
               [0, 0,  I_m, -I_m, -1],         #  t - u - sigma <= 0
               [0, 0, -I_m, -I_m, -1],         # -t - u - sigma <= 0
               [A_ineq, 0, 0, 0, 0],           #  p(x) <= cutoff
               [A_out,  0, 0, 0, 0]])          #  p(g) <= log N(g; 0, I)
A_eq = hstack([A_eq_pts, 0, -I_m, 0, 0])       #  A lam - t = log f
res = linprog(c, A_ub, b_ub, A_eq, b_eq, bounds=bounds, method="highs")

3.3 Choosing C

C trades sparsity against fit. The MATLAB code sweeps \(C\in\{0.01,0.1,25,\dots,10^4\}\) and stops at the first C where the residual at the highest sample exceeds 0.01 % of its value, keeping the previous solution. If even the smallest C violates that tolerance, it keeps the first solution.

For CUT-8 the peak residual is \(10^{-6}\) % up to C = 0.1 and then jumps, so the rule picks C = 0.1 with 15 of 28 coefficients nonzero. For both grids the peak residual is about 100 % for every C. The rule never fires and falls back to C = 0.01.

5 · Why: tensor grids cannot see six polynomials

A 5-node grid has only five distinct values per axis. The degree-5 node polynomial

$$ \omega(x)=\prod_{k=1}^{5}(x-x_k)=x\,(x^2-a^2)(x^2-b^2) $$

is zero at every grid node, and so is anything it multiplies. Within total degree 6 that gives six independent polynomials:

$$ \mathcal N=\operatorname{span}\{\ \omega(x),\ x\,\omega(x),\ y\,\omega(x),\ \omega(y),\ y\,\omega(y),\ x\,\omega(y)\ \},\qquad A\,\boldsymbol\lambda_{\mathcal N}=\mathbf 0 . $$

So the 25 × 28 design matrix has rank \(28-6=22\), not 25. That has two consequences.

  1. The grid can't interpolate. Only 22 of the 25 equations are independent. Unless the data happen to lie in the range of \(A\), no degree-6 polynomial passes through all 25 samples. For the test surface, the best least-squares fit still has residuals up to 0.41 in log units.
  2. The L1-type residual concentrates the misfit. \(\operatorname{norm\_largest}\) with \(k=21\) is close to an \(\ell_1\) norm on the residuals, and \(\ell_1\) solutions are sparse. The LP satisfies 23 of the 25 equations exactly and puts nearly all of the misfit on one sample: the peak.

CUT-8 doesn't have this problem. Its points are not a product of 1D sets, and its 21 × 28 design matrix has full row rank (21), so the equality constraints can always be met exactly. The singular values make the difference stark:

This is a degree threshold, not a gradual effect. \(\omega\) has degree 5, so with \(N_m\le4\) the grids have no null space and behave well. For \(N_m\ge 5\), with a \(q\)-node tensor grid that becomes \(N_m\ge q\), they become rank deficient. The Gaussian banana shows it cleanly: its log is exactly degree 4, and all three point sets recover it exactly at degree 4 or 5. At degree 6, CUT-8 still recovers it exactly, while both grids have 6–7 % error on a surface the basis can represent perfectly.

5.1 Is the L1 LP the right tool?

The diagnosis suggests a check: replace the LP with plain minimum-norm least squares on the same points and basis, with no constraints at all.

On the grids, least squares halves the error (6.9 % and 6.5 %), because spreading an unavoidable misfit is better than concentrating it at the peak. On CUT-8, where there is no unavoidable misfit, the L1 LP wins (4.1 % vs 4.9 %): its sparsity prior picks a better interpolant from the 7-dimensional family that fits all 21 samples, and its constraints keep the tails sensible. So the L1 LP and CUT points work well together, and the L1 LP and a tensor grid at \(N_m\ge q\) do not.

7 · Where the comparison is fair, and where it isn't

  • The surface was chosen to show the effect. It is unimodal with a sharp, curved peak, which is the regime where the null-space failure hurts most. On a three-component Gaussian mixture with separate peaks, all three sets fail at 21–25 points (12–14 % at their best degree, CUT-8 slightly ahead). On very smooth, nearly Gaussian surfaces, the 25-point grids pull slightly ahead of CUT-8 at higher degree, simply because they have more points.
  • The scaling convention matters. Scaling every set to the same box (§2.4) concentrates CUT-8 toward the center. The original code also transforms points to the density's estimated mean and covariance before fitting. This port leaves that step out so the three sets can be compared on equal terms.
  • The grid failure is avoidable. Keep \(N_m<q\) for a \(q\)-node tensor grid, use least squares instead of an L1 residual, or use a non-tensor design such as a Smolyak sparse grid or CUT. The lesson is about pairing: an \(\ell_1\)-type residual assumes the system can be solved exactly, and a tensor grid quietly breaks that assumption once the degree reaches the number of nodes per axis.
  • Exactness is not the mechanism. Both rules integrate every monomial of the degree-6 basis exactly against a Gaussian. The fit, however, is an interpolation problem, and the property that matters is that the basis is unisolvent on the points. CUT-8 has it; tensor grids lose it at \(N_m\ge q\).
